1. nicolaguglielmi/lights-out: turning "let AI write the code" into a lights-out factory — eight numeric gates judge every PR, every ticket gets its own perimeter, a bad canary rolls itself back, and the last step always needs one human GO

URL: https://github.com/nicolaguglielmi/lights-out ｜ ★6 ｜ Apache-2.0 ｜ Shell ｜ The full guardrail system for AI coding agents: a spec gate, eight numeric gates, per-ticket perimeters, a canary that rolls itself back, and a single human GO

What happened

61 commits inside the window (11-07 02:30Z to 11-08 00:50Z), whose thread is turning "trust the agent" into "have numeric gates judge every layer". The README opens bluntly: "The safety system for a dark factory of AI coding agents. A lights-out factory runs in the dark because its safety systems are trusted."

  • ⭐ Eight gates, each printing one line of GO or NO-GO with the reason: risk, secrets, weakened tests, dependencies, static analysis, tests, mutation, and test-proof. The test-proof gate is the most interesting definition: "a test the change adds must fail on the base" — in other words it does not check whether the tests pass, but whether the test has any power at all. The README also states that every threshold says where it comes from, and that "A PR is judged by its base's policy, never its own" — a PR is judged by the baseline branch's policy, never by the policy it introduces itself.
  • ⭐ A perimeter per ticket: the diff must stay inside the files the ticket declares, checked before a PR even exists; a ticket whose files another open ticket holds waits at assignment. This turns "the agent quietly touched something else" from a habit into a structural constraint.
  • ⭐ A canary that rolls itself back with nobody paged: each merge produces a signed image that first takes 5% of real traffic, then 25%, compared against stable on errors and p95 latency; a bad number rolls it back on its own. The README puts it precisely: "A bad number rolls it back with nobody paged."
  • ⭐ The single human gate follows one rule: "One human gate — the step to 100% waits for a person's GO. Silence never promotes." In other words silence is not a promotion — an absent human does not push the rollout to full traffic.
  • ⭐ Main stays green without a queue: a PR whose perimeter did not move merges without a second judgment; after merging, main is judged again, and a red main is bisected and the culprit reverted, sending its ticket back to rework.
  • ⭐ A remarkably honest boundary statement: the README says "An anchored timeline is not a true one: the check stops invented releases and log lines, not a wrong story about real ones." It can stop fabricated release records and log lines; it cannot stop a distorted account of real ones.
  • ⭐ The engineering details are spelled out too: the container image is 1.8 GB unpacked, and after the first pull doctor, init and gates took 65 s and 72 s; a full demo run takes about 7 minutes (434 s measured on a MacBook with podman), with 3-6 minutes for the first toolchain build; the image is unsigned and its tags are mutable, so pinning requires a digest (LIGHTS_OUT_IMAGE=...@sha256:d647d055...); and the wrapper mounts your repository read-only and never hands the image your engine's socket.

Why it matters

  • ⭐ This is very nearly the complete answer to something I have been looking for over the past two years. My 4P precision nutrition platform is Flask + SQLite, where the AI recomputes an entire intervention plan and writes it to the database; the GLP-1 nutrition companion is a Python recipe engine that directly emits a morning and evening formula; GRPM reports are structured conclusions produced by an LLM. All three places currently lack a runtime layer that decides "on what grounds may this conclusion be stored." lights-out's eight gates are exactly that layer in executable form. The test-proof gate is the one I intend to copy directly: every risk dimension in my intervention report should ship with a test that must fail against the old data, otherwise that risk dimension is decoration.
  • ⭐ "A PR is judged by the base's policy, never its own" hits precisely the bug type that hurts me most. In my intervention report pipeline, if a change happens to loosen one validation, the next report is judged by the loosened rule — and once the rule has been broken, every later output is laundered, with nobody noticing. Putting the validation rules into a versioned policy file separate from the generation logic, and requiring rule changes to go through their own review, should become a hard rule in the 4P platform.
  • ⭐ "Silence never promotes" is the line I most want to copy into content production. In my current workflow, if I am away for three days without looking, AI-generated and AI-published content is by default already published. lights-out's approach is: without a human GO, it stops at 25% — no rollback, no promotion. That maps exactly onto the hard red line I keep for myself — never claim medical credentials — which in engineering terms means "without human confirmation, nothing goes public."
  • ⭐ The canary compares "error rate + p95 latency", and that metric choice transfers directly. When I ship a new version of my platform, what should be watched is "lab-report OCR extraction failure rate" and "report generation p95 latency", not "test pass rate" — the latter barely moves once there is an AI fallback.
  • ⭐ The sentence about anchored timelines not being true timelines should be copied into the footnote of every report I publish. My most common error in evidence-based writing is not fabrication but stretching one real but isolated piece of evidence into a stronger claim than it supports. A tool can block fake citations; it cannot block over-extension — those are two different things that must be declared separately, which is the same principle as the "association must not be upgraded to causation" rule I recorded in an earlier issue.
  • Apache-2.0, readable, modifiable and commercially usable, and it runs on your own machine (podman or docker; no account, no API key, no cloud). MIT and Apache-2.0 account for 130 of this issue's 158 entries; AGPL-3.0 accounts for 6 (OwnHealth, get-based, OpenMontage, dental-crm is all-rights-reserved, SuperMedicalDoctor and others) and can be read but not copied — that row has to be read before you choose a base.

2. Rul1an/assay: an offline-recomputable evidence profile for privileged tool calls — it states plainly that "a tool returning success is the provider's assertion, never proof", and grades claims into four tiers

URL: https://github.com/Rul1an/assay ｜ ★16 ｜ MIT ｜ Rust ｜ A policy-enforcement and evidence layer at the MCP tool-call boundary: Enforce / Prove / Stay honest, with claims graded verified, self-reported, inferred or absent

What happened

16 commits inside the window, whose thread is the v6.22.3 release (11-07 08:22Z), described in one line: "Deterministic policy enforcement, canonical evidence, and reviewable trust artifacts for AI agent systems."

  • ⭐ Its reason for existing is stated plainly: "Agents got real tool access through MCP — and tool poisoning, rug pulls, and confused-deputy OAuth came with it." So it sits at the tool-call boundary and does three things, in a fixed order.
  • ⭐ Enforce: in enforcement mode, the proxy decides tools/call requests before forwarding them, and gives the precise reason for every allow or deny. On supported Linux hosts there are separate controls: cgroup eBPF IPv4/TCP connect filtering and Landlock TCP-connect port allowlisting; assay sandbox --enforce refuses to start when the backend is unavailable or the filesystem policy conflicts, unless --allow-audit-fallback is explicit; --enforce-net requires --enforce, and even with audit fallback enabled, active Landlock network enforcement still rejects policies it cannot express as TCP port allowlists.
  • ⭐ Prove: configured producers can record decisions and bounded observations for export into offline-verifiable, tamper-evident evidence bundles (tar.gz) that CI can consume directly; assay evidence prepare-incident-package turns a decision log into an incident package that verify-incident-package accepts, and the verifier derives the surface grade itself.
  • ⭐ Stay honest is the part most worth copying. Trust Basis classifies supported claims as verified, self_reported, inferred or absent, and its gates check whether the declared claim boundaries have been crossed. The README's own wording: "A tool returning 'success' is the provider's assertion, never proof." And: "Assay ships no single safety score; read each artifact's source, coverage and non-claims before relying on it." It refuses to output one overall score and requires you to read the artifact's source, coverage and non-claims first.
  • ⭐ It also states, in a dedicated list, what it can and cannot prove: the evidence package is not a count of all calls, not proof of capture completeness, not proof of what the server did, and an allow is not proof that a human accepted.
  • ⭐ Installation and supply chain are hardened too: the one-liner installer verifies the selected archive against its published SHA-256 sidecar before extraction; setting ASSAY_REQUIRE_PROVENANCE=1 additionally requires GitHub artifact provenance, while the default reports provenance_not_requested and a strict success reports provenance_verified. The README adds the crucial caveat: a checksum proves byte equality with the published sidecar, not producer identity; provenance identifies source and build, not runtime safety or semantic correctness.
  • ⭐ The release cadence is dense: v6.22.3 (11-07), v6.22.2 (11-06), v6.22.1 (11-05) — one release per day for three consecutive days, evidence of sustained engineering rather than a one-off demo.

Why it matters

  • ⭐ "A tool returning success is the provider's assertion, never proof" should become the literal precondition for writing to my 4P platform. My lab-report OCR pipeline currently decides "extract succeeded, write it; extraction failed, skip it" — which is exactly equivalent to treating "the extractor says it read a value" as "that item is really on the lab report." The correct shape, per assay, is that every extraction must carry a four-tier claim (verified = read from a checkable position on the page; self_reported = the OCR engine's own assertion; inferred = derived from context; absent = never obtained), and absent is not normal. That is structurally identical to the "a check that was not performed is recorded as unknown, never as normal" rule I recorded in an earlier issue — but assay turns it into machine-readable schema.
  • ⭐ "Refusing to output a single safety score" is the design judgment I most want to take away from this issue. In an earlier issue I recorded Calibrate replacing a single score with a 2x2 dependency matrix; this issue's assay achieves the same thing with "four claim tiers plus no overall score." Applied to my GRPM reports: a risk conclusion for an SNP must never be "risk score 72"; it must be a chain that can be audited piece by piece — "this site is variant XX (verified, source: Sanger report, page 3, column 2) → judged as XXX under GRPM rules (self_reported) → this site was not tested in this individual (absent)". Readers may see a score, but the copy in the database must be tiered.
  • ⭐ The "non-claims" concept can become a fixed chapter in my content production. What I most often lack in evidence-based articles is an explicit statement of what the evidence cannot say — assay writes that into the artifact itself. Applied to GRPM's SNP interpretation: "the evidence linking this site to folate metabolism is moderate strength; it cannot be used to predict your actual dose response to folate supplementation" — one sentence kills most over-extension.
  • ⭐ Its implementation of "a third party can recompute the evidence offline" is the same thread I recorded with aureproof, but this time the verifier itself also supplies the coverage grade — meaning "what this evidence covers and does not cover" is produced by the verifying side rather than declared by the generating side. That is the concrete form of the "GRPM reports must be independently recomputable offline" requirement I noted earlier.**
  • ⭐ MIT, commercially usable. The cost is that it is implemented in Rust, so integration takes some engineering. But it exposes a standard CLI and a CI Action (Rul1an/assay-action@v3), so in my case the far more likely usage is "run the verification once in CI" rather than embedding the proxy into the runtime — a much lower barrier.

9 commits inside the window, whose thread is the 0.1.0 release (the first version you can actually run) plus the security design document. The most telling commit title is: "docs: stop genome lookups and page reads from revealing withheld findings, and match re-derivation to the shipped code".

Why it matters

  • ⭐ This is the piece that fits my business best — and it is very nearly written for the "never claim medical credentials" problem. Why it matters

  • ⭐ "Path-scoped rules load only when that layer is touched" is a cost optimization I can apply directly to my expert packages. I produce expert packages in bulk with the expert-manager skill, and each one is a SKILL.md that must be read in full on every call — while in most expert packages well over half the content (implementation details of one particular layer, the usage of one particular backend framework) is needed by only a minority of tasks. samd-starter-kit's layered rule loading is the "five-layer placement" idea from skill-diet, implemented at file granularity. This should go into my expert-package spec: anything that can be loaded on demand must not be merged into one large file.
  • ⭐ "Analyze impact before fixing" (§5.6) treats an old disease of my GRPM and 4P platforms. When I change an SNP decision rule, I normally verify only the few lines that reference it directly — while the GRPM report output, the Excel export and the public-account article each carry their own copy of code referencing the same fields, and changing one usually leaves the other two unaware. samd's answer is to make impact analysis a skill that must run first, not something the author remembers. This is the same problem addressed by a different method from the "write the answer contract once and assemble both prompts from it" note I recorded last issue: one is structural alignment, the other is process enforcement.
  • ⭐ "Mutation tests as contracts" and the score ratchet are ready-made methods for my supplement formula library. When I change one threshold in my formula logic (say a contraindication rule), no regression test currently tells me that the change altered the behaviour of seven other rules. The Stryker-style approach is to deliberately break the code and see whether your tests catch it — if the tests do not catch it, that rule is effectively untested. This is the same idea as lights-out's test-proof gate (a newly added test must fail on the base), implemented two different ways.
  • ⭐ "Identity comes only from the token, never a user_id from the client" is a rule I must copy. My 4P platform is Flask + SQLite with multiple users; if any endpoint reads user_id from the request body to decide whose lab data to query, that is a ready-made unauthorized-read path. The cost is close to zero — just make "user_id may only be derived from the session or token" a non-bypassable rule and add one test for it.
  • ⭐ Turning "compliance" from documentation into CI gates is a methodology for the vendor-qualification and project-compliance work I am currently doing. My previous approach was to hand a compliance document to the other side; this approach makes each requirement a CI check that can go red — a document can be skipped, a check cannot. Applied to my situation: every security promise my 4P platform and AI assistant make should have a test that can go red, not a paragraph that asserts it.
  • ⭐ MIT licensed and commercially usable, and it says outright that "This repository is not an application. It is the scaffolding and methodology" — it exists to be forked and adapted.

3. Trends today

The clearest thread this issue is that constraints are sinking from "requirements written in a prompt" down into "gates that cannot be bypassed at runtime", and the gates increasingly carry three things: where the threshold came from, the reason for the verdict, and a record of the refusal. lights-out's eight gates each print one line of GO/NO-GO and cite the threshold's source; assay gives the precise reason for every allow or deny before forwarding and writes the decision into an offline-recomputable evidence bundle; samd-starter-kit writes "no pushing without explicit consent" into hard rules; OwnHealth uses token scopes so that what you may not see cannot leak even indirectly. Four unrelated projects, all answering the same question: not "what should the AI do", but "when the AI oversteps, how does the system stop it, and is there a trace a third party can re-check afterwards." This is the fifth stage of the thread I recorded with ClawBio's skill-maturity grading and navigator's refusal to end a turn: the first four stages pushed constraints down into code and configuration; this stage pushes them down into the runtime and the supply chain, and demands that the runtime emit auditable evidence.

The second thread is that the unit of cost measurement keeps getting finer, and this time it reaches the level of model choice. Last issue skill-diet reported "70-80% of tokens cut with no drop in test results"; this issue sprag reports "the same 12 requests, $6.68 to $1.83 (−72.6%)", and states explicitly why one task must stay on Sonnet high rather than dropping to Haiku — because replays showed the default medium effort missed it. Together these mean the cost-effectiveness of an AI artifact must now be measured as a triple: on which model, burning how many tokens, passing how many tasks — and the third component needs a recomputable mechanism such as replay plus blind grading, or cost reduction is a gamble. For someone like me who runs the same batch of content-production tasks across several models, the preconditions for replay verification already exist on this machine (WorkBuddy is wired to a single OpenRouter key covering the whole model family); I have simply never used them.

The third thread, and the one that should make me most alert this issue, is that health and medical projects are collectively splitting "I don't know" out of a null value into a set of named fields. OwnHealth's response envelope carries not_assessed (never evaluated), withheld (deliberately not disclosed), limitations (the boundary of the conclusion) and evidence (every item with src); assay grades claims as verified / self_reported / inferred / absent and refuses to emit an overall score; trial-matcher outputs met / not met / unknown and says plainly that its heuristic score is uncalibrated; iatheon-verified-errors ties each verified error in AI-written oncology summaries back to its primary source. This is the fourth stage of the same "structure the evidence" thread: evidence is not prose, absence is not blank, and unknown is not a denial. My OCR pipeline, my GRPM reports and my 4P intervention reports all do "AI produces a structured conclusion, then it is written down" — and in all three places, "we did not get it" is currently written as a null value. That is the first thing I should change.

The fourth thread: licensing continues to evolve from "one LICENSE file" toward "layered + routed + three separate declarations", and the non-commercial category is visibly growing. Of this issue's 158 entries, MIT and Apache-2.0 account for 130 (82%), but there are also six AGPL-3.0, one Elastic-2.0, one PolyForm, two all-rights-reserved, one custom Databricks license, and nine repos confirmed to have no LICENSE file at all. What is most worth noting about OwnHealth is that it does not merely pick AGPL-3.0: it actively uses that license to underwrite its "never a hosted or paid version" non-commercial positioning, and argues from it that it is not a GDPR controller. That is a clear signal — when a project welds its license to its business model, the users it wants and the users it excludes are equally clear. For someone like me who intends to "pick a base and build something sellable", this list belongs on the first line of the selection document.

The fifth thread, and a direct note to Gordon: the five instances of "one bug, five different fixes" I counted in this issue all point at the same unfixed problem — an AI-generated conclusion being treated as a fact that may be written to disk. lights-out's test-proof gate guarantees "a newly added test must fail on the base"; assay requires every claim to state whether it is verified or self-reported; OwnHealth gives "not assessed" and "withheld" a field each and marks every PDF text layer as untrusted; samd-starter-kit refuses to declare anything green without running the linked tests; sprag requires every cost-saving rule to have a replay case you can open. Five teams on five completely different tech stacks, independently, in the same week, all doing the same thing: making the AI's words stand on their own. And my three outputs — the 4P platform database, the GRPM report export, the public-account article — have none of that check today. Concretely, four things are needed: ① every extraction carries a four-tier claim; ② missing items go to not_assessed rather than to a null; ③ read back by primary key after writing and block downstream on mismatch; ④ any claim of "covering N dimensions" must be counted by a script, not asserted by a model. None of these four needs a new framework — only four assertions inside the existing pipeline.
