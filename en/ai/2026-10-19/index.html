4. xinxuxin/keystone-bench — clinical evaluation asks a different question

Repo: https://github.com/xinxuxin/keystone-bench ｜ ★808

Instead of "is this clinical answer correct", it asks "when the evidence changes, does your action change": give the same patient scenario with evidence of varying quality and direction, then watch whether the recommended workup, prescription or referral moves accordingly.

A self-hosted AI health data engine that ingests lab reports, wearables and genetic data into traceable answers, with a built-in MCP server, Docker deployment plus your own LLM key, or a fully local llama.cpp run.

Trends

  1. Official skill supply appears. android/skills is the first time a platform vendor defines agent skills from its own repository. Community skills solve "does it exist"; official skills solve "dare we ship it".
  2. Agent engineering pivots to reliability. The infrastructure list — task-state-guard (state reconciliation), cayu (crash recovery and approvals), maka (append-only audit), astrid (capability sandbox) — all answer one question: what happens when an agent dies halfway through. That is the debt every demo must pay before production.
  3. Medical AI evaluation enters an "evidence sensitivity" stage: from correct (HealthBench) to explainable (auditable) to "does your action track the evidence" (keystone). The evaluation axis is converging on how clinical decisions actually work.
  4. Nutrition projects converge on local-first plus judgement. fud-ai, nutritrace and others are zero-telemetry and self-hosted, expanding from calorie logging to additive scoring, allergen detection and supplement evidence. Privacy is no longer a selling point but an entry ticket.
  5. Noise worth ignoring. The nutrition and healthcare-ai topics now carry many low-quality SEO repositories — the same pubmed-mcp-server copied several times in a day, template sites like NourishAI-Forge. Look at commit history and issue quality, not star counts.
