1. "Does your skill actually help?" can finally be measured: Intel's dokimi runs paired A/B tests on agent skills

URL: https://github.com/intel/dokimi ｜ ★39 ｜ Apache-2.0 ｜ An A/B testing framework for agent context: paired on/off-arm experiments that judge whether a skill, MCP doc source, agent profile or repository guidance actually improves output

What happened

  • 30 commits today, with the line of work being the cleanup of a "trace/bench wave" that had not finished: fix(dokimi): leftovers of the trace/bench wave (#1199), feat(catalog): rank the novelty sweep by new share (#846), fix(repo-scan): a code span naming the repository's own owner/name is ok (#1252) and fix(repo): a cd after the first command of an if or loop body may not run (#1182). Taken together these commits are about making the measurement tool itself correct at the edges, not about adding features.
  • The question it exists to answer is stated plainly: "Does this context help?" The method is to run the same question or coding task under different context conditions, compare the outcomes, and then investigate where the difference came from.
  • Its capabilities are split into 39 individual feature cards, each of which states what works today and where it stops. The README is explicit that open pull requests, partial workflows and research directions are all included, but are labelled as unreleased so they are discoverable without being advertised as shipped functionality.
  • For "which part of a skill helps", it uses novelty: it asks the model about each claim in a skill twice — once without the skill and once with it — and a different model grades both answers, with every claim landing in one of four outcomes.
  • It separates several questions cleanly: whether the skill is actually loaded (discovery, routing, tool preference, instruction loading), whether repository guidance works (static claims, scopes, executable tasks, rule evidence), and whether the grades can be trusted (scoring methods, judge checks, grounding).
  • The two most consequential lines in the README are its own caveats: "Read the measured delta, uncertainty and task-level evidence together; one passing example does not establish a general benefit." and "A skill can teach something new without improving a task, and an instruction can exist without being loaded." — it explicitly separates "taught something new" from "made the task better", and explicitly separates "the instruction exists" from "the instruction was loaded".

Why it matters

  • This one directly addresses the thing I most want to do with my own expert packages and skill packs, and have never had a method for: proving that they actually work. I build expert packages with WorkBuddy's expert-manager skill, and I have a pile of self-made skills. But up to now my judgement of whether a skill is good rests on "does it feel right when I use it" — that is an impression, not evidence. dokimi supplies exactly the missing piece: run the same task set with the arm on and the arm off, and report the measured delta and the uncertainty. Applied to my own project, that means a paired experiment on my "4P Precision Nutrition Watch" content pipeline: with the skill pack versus without it, comparing missed-citation rate, section completeness and elapsed time. Once I have that number, my skill pack stops being "I think it helps" and becomes "measurably effective at N%".
  • "One passing example does not establish a general benefit" is the same discipline as what I recorded last issue from Empirica — "weaker evidence cannot carry a higher confidence" — in a second form. I have been holding myself to "only cite conclusions traceable to a primary source". That rule runs in the other direction too: a tool that passes one case does not mean the skill helps on every task. I intend to write this straight into my skill acceptance criteria — look at the mean, the uncertainty and the task-level evidence together; a single-point success does not count.
  • "An instruction can exist without being loaded" hits a blind spot I genuinely have. I have been assuming that once I put a rule into SKILL.md, the agent will follow it. dokimi breaks out discovery, routing, tool preference and instruction loading into separate dimensions precisely because "I wrote it" and "it read it and did it" are two different things. I can point this at my own skill pack: of the "mandatory evidence plus references" I enforce, what proportion actually reaches the output? This way of measuring is far more reliable than my impression that "it seems to be complying".
  • Making "can the grades be trusted" its own layer is more rigorous than most evaluation tools. The README is candid: "Grader controls help expose blind spots; they do not prove a verifier is infallible." — the judge itself can be wrong. That means it will not hand me "87% pass rate" and call it done; it keeps asking how that 87% was computed. That attitude is worth more to me than any number it produces.
  • Honestly stated: it has 39 stars and 30 commits today, and the README repeatedly bounds its own claims, including noting that a particular experiment "measures retrieval of the known-answer passage, not generated-answer quality". So my position is: it is the only open-source project I have seen that treats "does the skill help" as a measurable question at all — but its output still has to be validated against my own task set. I will use it to test my skill pack; I will not treat its score as an evaluation of my product — it measures "on a given task set", not "across my whole business".

3. Every citation can be clicked open to its source: TraceableAI turns the PRISMA systematic review into a verifiable pipeline

URL: https://github.com/lauPhilip/au-btech-literature-review-agent ｜ ★2 ｜ Apache-2.0 ｜ A literature-review agent from Aarhus University's BTECH: runs a full PRISMA 2020 systematic review and checks every citation sentence by sentence against the original, marking it green, orange, or not supported

What happened

  • 100 commits today plus v0.6.0, making it one of the highest-commit-volume projects in this issue. Today's work sits on two lines: merging pull requests and filling in the changelog (Merge pull request #694 from lauPhilip/docs/changelog-688-690 among them), and fixing functionality (fix/selfcheck-and-search-cap, fix/report-page-paging).
  • It writes the review protocol before it searches anything, then screens, then writes — and only the last step is self-checking the citations. The README flow chart spells this out: A[Artifact] → C[Citation check and repair] → R[PRISMA report + verifiable archive].
  • The unit of judgement is the sentence, not the paper: for every cited sentence it has to find the passage in the paper that supports it, and say plainly when it cannot. Verdicts fall into three grades — supported (the paper says it) / partly supported / not supported — with green and orange used in the report. "A sentence that claims more than its paper is rewritten to fit the quote and checked again" — a sentence overreaching its source gets pulled back to what the quote supports and re-checked; a citation the paper does not back at all is removed, along with its claim.
  • "Two of everything": every record is screened twice by two independent prompts, themes are assigned twice, and agreement is reported as Cohen's kappa — the same yardstick human reviewers use.
  • "The methods are facts, not prose": the methods section is generated from what the run actually did, so it never describes a database that was not searched or a step that did not happen.
  • "Everything is on file": the protocol, raw database responses, every screening decision with its reasoning, every model call and the citation audit are all saved and fingerprinted with SHA-256, downloadable as one archive containing a ready-to-compile LaTeX manuscript plus BibTeX/RIS files.
  • It draws its own boundary: the README says "TraceableAI is a co-pilot for a human reviewer, not a replacement. Its checks make errors findable; they do not make them impossible."

Why it matters

  • This one closes the most concrete and most damaging hole in my content production. When I cite literature in a WeChat article, readers see a reference list — but nobody can tell whether I had actually read the paper when I made that claim, or only skimmed the abstract. TraceableAI turns that into a product feature: every citation carries its source passage and verdict, clickable. My readers are people making decisions straight off my conclusions, and what they need is exactly that route back to the source.
  • The rule about a sentence claiming more than its paper is precisely the mistake I make most often when relaying research. My common failure mode: the original says "a correlation was observed in a small sample", and I write "research proves". It converts that into a machine-checkable rewrite rule — any part exceeding the source must be pulled back to what the source supports. I can run this as an automated pre-publication check on my own drafts.
  • The Cohen's kappa detail is what lifted my confidence in it. Most AI review tools report a single "citation accuracy" number; it reports inter-rater agreement — the methodological standard of human literature review, not an AI-tool KPI. A project willing to measure itself by the reviewers' yardstick is signalling that it knows what it is doing.
  • "Everything is on file + SHA-256 fingerprints" maps directly onto my own "Hundred-Person Project" data. I hold observer-indicator analysis from that 2022–2025 cohort, and if I publish it, the thing I most fear is that what I say cannot be traced back to that data. Its approach: archive the raw responses, every decision with its reasoning, and every call, all fingerprinted. That layer is precisely what my project documentation system is missing.
  • It applies its own "the methods are facts, not prose" rule, and that is worth copying. In the intervention documents I write there is a "research methods" section — and if that section was written to the ideal flow rather than to what I actually did, it is a dishonest document.
  • But it has 2 stars, and it says outright that it cannot replace a human reviewer, only make errors findable. The distinction I need to keep straight is the one it draws itself: a citation being real is not the same as that paper supporting that sentence. These two things I have also been conflating. My conclusion: borrow its verdict mechanism and its archiving discipline; do not treat its three-colour marking as "verified".

5. The evidence ledger became executable scripts: ai-research-workflow uses a five-layer audit and a "hallucination rate" to stop AI fabrication

URL: https://github.com/zhuowenzhang288-web/ai-research-workflow ｜ ★2 ｜ MIT ｜ A phenomenon-driven, cross-platform research-workflow skill: novelty evidence, a topic funnel, an experiment log, citation verification and a five-layer audit, with 8 zero-dependency scripts, working on Claude Code / Codex / Cursor / opencode / DSH

What happened

  • 47 commits today plus v1.0.0 (its first release). The commit messages are themselves written in Chinese, and the line of work is finishing and consistency-checking: a script that checks consistency across the three copies of the project, a fix for duplicated section numbering 0.1/0.2, a relaxation of venue_probe so it is no longer restricted to top-tier venues, the addition of sync.sh, and a gitignore update for generated artefacts; plus a README table-of-contents update adding sync.sh and gen_architecture.py.
  • Its core claim is "reverse the order": the traditional flow is think of an idea → check novelty → run experiments; but an agent's ability to invent ideas out of thin air is weak, so it flips to running several hundred second-long experiments in a small system (0.1–4M parameters) to find regularities first, then reverse-engineering the idea from the regularity. Its own ordering rule: "if you can go phenomenon-driven, don't go literature-driven; if you can go literature-driven, don't invent out of thin air."
  • The five-layer audit chain is judged by "objectively falsifiable, decided with tools and rules, not guessed by an LLM": ① fabricated citation (the citation is findable at the official source with a matching title; any wrong citation count above zero is a FAIL) ② misattribution (the paper exists but is described wrongly) ③ fabricated number (every key number needs a source anchor) ④ fabricated result (every result must point back to a run.log / results.tsv / commit) ⑤ fabricated status (status wording must correspond one-to-one with the log).
  • The citation-verification script outputs a hallucination rate HR, defined as "wrong citations / total citations", with the rule "HR > 0 means FAIL".
  • Three rounds of novelty checking, and "abstract-level reading cannot support a KEEP/KILL verdict": the report must label each reference's reading depth (abstract-level / body section / full text), and a missing label is a FAIL.
  • "Do not review your own work": an independent reviewer may read only the original artifact or the source text, never the executor's summary or intermediate reasoning; a self-review from the same model family counts only as provisional.
  • It folds its own failures into its discipline: 26 literature-gap directions from a search-driven approach with zero survivors, one direction that died after five successive narrow variants, two evaluation bugs that burned 40+ GPU-hours — hence the rule "narrow ≠ safe; decisively changing direction beats drilling a crack".
  • All 8 scripts are pure Python standard library with no third-party dependencies; there is also a double-insurance layer of AGENTS.md (a condensed version of the core discipline, auto-injected on every request) and SKILL.md (the full methodology, loaded on demand).

Why it matters

  • "HR > 0 means FAIL" is a stricter rule than any writing standard I have. I have said "zero AI hallucination", but my actual test has been "I checked each one and found no fake citation" — that is sampling, not zero tolerance. It fixes the definition as "wrong citations / total citations" and says that a single wrong citation fails the whole piece. I accept that standard completely and should accept it — because one wrong citation collapses the credibility of an entire piece of content; there is no "mostly correct".
  • The "fabricated number" and "fabricated result" layers are what I need most when writing nutrition intervention plans. Those plans contain concrete figures — a supplement dose, the size of an expected improvement in a marker. I require myself that "the data must have a source", but I have never turned "can this number in the plan point back to a specific row of my experiment log" into a runnable check. Its audit_chain.py verify-results <report>.md command does exactly that: matching the numbers in the report against the rows in the log.
  • "Abstract-level reading cannot support a KEEP/KILL verdict" is directly usable as a criterion for my own literature relaying. When I judge in an article that "this study shows X", in the overwhelming majority of cases I have read only the abstract. Its rule translates to: a conclusion drawn from abstract-level reading cannot be used as a conclusion, only as a lead. I intend to put that sentence on my writing standards wall.
  • Its "separation of execution and review" is my "independent check" — except I have no tool for it. I currently review my own drafts, which is not methodologically sound, and I know it. Its mechanism is: use a different model, isolate the context, hand it only the raw source, and have it judge SUPPORTED / UNSUPPORTED. That is entirely implementable in WorkBuddy — open a fresh session, supply only the original text, and let it judge without the intermediate reasoning.
  • Its failure memory and anti-narrowing mechanisms cure two habits of mine specifically. My "4P Precision Nutrition Thousand-Person Pioneer Project" SOP never records failed directions, only the ones that worked — so in six months I will try the same dead end again. 3. Trends today

  • "Does it actually help" is becoming a measurable question rather than a gut feeling. Three projects today ran into the same problem from different directions: intel/dokimi uses paired A/B experiments to test whether a piece of context improved output; zhuowenzhang288-web/ai-research-workflow uses citation verification plus a hallucination rate where HR > 0 means FAIL to test whether a claim has evidence; lauPhilip/au-btech-literature-review-agent compares each cited sentence against the source to test whether a citation holds its sentence up. All three replace "this looks right" with "run it and look at the number". For me, taken together, these three form a complete self-check chain — produce → independent check → quantify the delta → keep the uncertainty.
  • Evidence grading is moving from binary toward three tiers plus traceable drill-down. u9401066/med-paper-assistant distinguishes sources with 🔒VERIFIED / 🤖AGENT / ✏️USER labels; lauPhilip marks every cited sentence green, orange or not-supported and hangs the source passage behind the number; PangenomeAI simply writes it as a hard rule — no full text means abstract-level reading, flagged as unverified. Three different shapes, but all answering the same question: when a reader clicks a citation, how much should they know? What matters most to me here is that grading is not a post-publication formality — it is part of the production pipeline, not a remedy.
  • The most interesting thing in line ③ today is not the products but a structural problem three repositories expose at once: where the data comes from, and whether there is only one source of it. mandalarigabriele/intake chooses to read live and never persist, and the cost is that its long-term history depends entirely on the single Nutrition service; gerardrecinto/robo-trainer-mcp states that food search is the only thing that leaves the machine, with training and nutrition data staying local; jare0014/obsidian-health-connect takes a third route — talking directly to the Google Health v4 REST API, and because Google has no write scope for mindfulness, mindfulness data can only stay in the local vault. Three projects, three different answers to "who owns the data" — and not one of them assumes it has only a single data source. That is a direct warning for my own "Hundred-Person Project" data handling: a single-source design is a risk for me, not a kind of simplicity I can afford.
  • "Local-first" has stopped being a slogan and become a specific architectural choice — but each choice carries its own price. juyterman1000/entroly pays for it by requiring that compression be recoverable: it issues a receipt for every piece of evidence and can return even omitted source text byte-exactly (README reports 66/66). angelnicolasc/graymatter pays by requiring memory to be exportable. kele551/health-db-skill pays by having its scripts default to not touching the network. mandalarigabriele/intake pays by not storing data at all. The phrase "local-first" by itself is no guarantee — it has to come with a mechanism that proves the data really is local and really is recoverable. Across these issues I am becoming more certain of this: any claim about privacy or locality needs a verifiable evidence channel, otherwise it is just copy.
  • Today's theme in line ④ is "saving" and "guardrails", not "getting smarter". entroly selects evidence within a token budget; graymatter claims a 90% token reduction; tokenx does high-accuracy token estimation in 2 kB; predikit bridges trained models into agents so you skip retraining; OpenAPPA provides deterministic guardrails; pi-warden intercepts destructive commands and secret exfiltration; QuesmaOrg curates token economics; jjiantong collects a KV-cache optimisation survey — eight lines all pointing at the same reality: the cost and the risk of an agent are now first-class citizens alongside its capability. For a one-person company with 8 hours a week and roughly a month of cash runway, this batch matters more to me than any new model.
